Chapter 19 · The whole chapter in three transactions

Somebody knows more

🚗You buy my used carI know more about its quality.
🩺I sell you health insuranceYou know more about your likely costs.
🧑‍💻I hire youYou know more about your effort.

The problem begins when the better-informed person can use that advantage—and our interests do not perfectly align.

First distinction

Private information is not just uncertainty

UncertaintyNobody knowsWill next winter be unusually cold?
Private informationSomebody knowsThe seller knows the car has been making that noise for weeks.

Diagnostic question: Who knows something consequential that somebody else cannot readily observe?

Chapter 19.1 · Four ways to create trust

Make quality visible—or make claims credible

1VerifyMechanics, inspectors, Carfax, reviews
2SignalWarranties, records, credentials
3DiscloseRequired reporting and truthful labels
4Set standardsLicensing and minimum quality

Policy question: Does the remedy help buyers distinguish quality—or merely add paperwork?

Chapter 19.2 · Every fix changes who enters

How might policy stabilize the pool?

1Broad poolingBring low- and high-risk people together
2Enrollment rulesLimit waiting until the need becomes obvious
3ScreeningUse observable information to sort risks
4SubsidiesKeep lower-risk people in the pool

Better pricing can improve incentives while weakening insurance, solidarity, privacy, or fairness.

The distinction to remember

Who you get—or what they do?

Adverse selectionHidden characteristicsPrivate information changes who participates or what quality is offered.Before or when the deal is made
Moral hazardHidden actionsProtection or delegation changes behavior that others cannot fully observe.After the deal is made

Chapter 19.3 · Principal and agent

Someone else acts on your behalf

🧑‍💼Employee effortThe employer cannot observe every choice.
🔧Mechanic adviceThe customer cannot verify every repair.
🏠Tenant careThe landlord cannot observe daily treatment.
🏦Expected rescueA safety net can encourage greater risk.
🩺Medical careInsurance lowers the price paid at the point of use.

Chapter 19.3 · Change the information or the payoff

Make good actions visible—or worthwhile

1MonitorAudits, inspections, supervision
2Share the stakesDeductibles, deposits, “skin in the game”
3Reward outcomesBonuses and performance contracts
4Redesign the jobMake desired actions easier to observe

No free lunch: monitoring is costly; cost-sharing weakens insurance; performance pay can invite gaming.

At work this week

Information problems are design problems

Do not begin by assuming bad motives. Begin by asking: Who knows what? Then design the market, contract, or institution around the answer.

PUBPOL 553 · Class 8: Game Theory

80 minutes · Prisoner’s Dilemma and coordination games first; the advanced material in a brisk final pass

Open slidesPrint / save as PDF

Tonight’s promise: game theory helps us predict what happens when people respond strategically—and helps policymakers change the rules rather than merely wishing players behaved differently.

Before class

Zoom setup
  • Keep chat open throughout.
  • Allow direct messages for the two games.
  • No poll setup is required.
  • Full-screen the webpage; arrows or space advance.
Your reminders
  • Use their work examples whenever possible.
  • Cold-call generously; these folks like to talk.
  • Ask “What would the other player do next?”
  • Do not equate equilibrium with “good.”

Run of class

TimeScreensWhat to do
0:00–0:061–2 · Opening“Put one problem from work in chat where your success depends on someone else’s choice.” Cold-call two. Identify players and choices; do not solve yet.Point: strategic actors react. They are boxers, not punching bags.
0:06–0:123–4 · ToolkitWalk through the chapter’s four steps. Promise two central problems: cooperation and coordination. “Nash means no one wants to change alone. It does not mean good.”
0:12–0:205 · Flood gamePick two volunteers as North and South County. Each sends you invest or free ride by direct message. Enter both choices and reveal simultaneously. Ask each: “What did you expect the other county to do?”
0:20–0:316–7 · Dilemma logicBuild the payoff table one step at a time. Ask the class for North’s best response in each column, then stand in South’s shoes. Reveal the Nash equilibrium and compare it with 3,3.Dwell here: individually rational behavior can create a collectively rotten result.
0:31–0:388 · Repeat itKeep the same volunteers. Tell them the number of rounds is unknown. Play 2–4 rounds; the page keeps score. Ask what changed once today’s move affected tomorrow’s treatment.
0:38–0:409 · RepetitionGive the short version: cooperation needs an uncertain future, observable behavior, and credible consequences. Do not linger on the formal mechanics.
0:40–0:4710 · Dilemma applicationsChoose two or three cases. Before clicking, ask students to name cooperation and defection. Use advertising, the commons, campaign spending, or the arms race. Include cartel undercutting if you want the important caveat: cooperation among firms can harm consumers. Finish with the data-standard decoy.
0:47–0:5311 · Phone tagPick two new volunteers. Each sends you call or wait by direct message. Reveal. If they miss, reset and try once more after allowing ten seconds of public communication.Point: interests align; expectations fail. There are two equilibria.
0:53–0:5912–13 · CoordinationContrast the two games. Then run the lunch-time exercise: everyone types 11:30, noon, or 12:30 but sends together. Reveal noon as the U.S. focal point. Ask for norms from their workplaces that perform the same function.
0:59–1:0514 · Bank runAsk for thumbs on “keep” versus “withdraw” under each expectation. Highlight both equilibria. Ask: “What does deposit insurance change—preferences, payoffs, or beliefs?”
1:05–1:0915 · SolutionsClick through communication, focal points/norms, and laws/regulations. Use road rules as the clean case.
1:09–1:1316 · Coordination applicationsChoose two or three cases. Political protest and skills/jobs show good and bad equilibria. Market entry shows anti-coordination. Bank runs and booms/busts connect strategic expectations to familiar policy problems.
1:13–1:1617 · Their workChoose one opening-chat problem. Fill in players, actions, and rough ordinal payoffs. Let the page mark best responses. Ask: “Is this a cooperation problem, a coordination problem, or neither?”
1:16–1:1918–19 · The restGive the chapter’s advanced material its cleanest summary: first mover = value of commitment; second mover = value of flexibility; repetition = value of future consequences. Do not get lost in tree mechanics unless asked.
1:19–1:2020 · Close“At work this week: name the players, choices, and equilibrium—then ask whether you can change the rules of the game.”

If time moves differently

If you are behind
  • Choose only one application on screens 10 and 16.
  • Use only the bank run—not the full work-game builder.
  • Cover screens 18–19 in one minute.
If you are ahead
  • Ask students for another Prisoner’s Dilemma from government or nonprofit work.
  • Build a second game from a student example.
  • Ask how communication, law, or a focal point could move a bad equilibrium.

Read the curve correctly

A supply curve is a set of plans.

It says how many assignments people would accept at each possible payment—not that all those payments occur.

Only one plan becomes today’s observed quantity supplied.

The chapter’s clean benchmark

Sometimes the supplier cannot choose the price.

A market price, Medicaid reimbursement, grant formula, salary schedule or procurement contract may simply post a rate.

If the price is given, the supplier’s choice is how much to provide at that price.

Marginal—even without a new invoice

New spending is not the same thing as marginal cost. “No new invoice” is not the same thing as “no cost.”

One more?

For a competitive, profit-seeking seller:

Supply one more unit whenprice ≥ marginal cost

Keep expanding until the next unit would cost more than the price received for it.

Use the benchmark—do not worship it

For governments and nonprofits, price may not be the entire benefit.

Mission, legal obligations and benefits to residents can justify supplying a service even when reimbursement alone does not cover its cost.

But the resources still have opportunity costs. Good intentions are not a technology for producing free Sundays.

What does the curve’s height mean?

The supply curve is the marginal-cost curve—under the chapter’s competitive benchmark.

Each extra unit has a marginal cost → the price required to make supplying it worthwhile

The curve’s height is the cost of the next unit—not the total cost of everything supplied.

Why might the fifth additional branch cost more to open than the first?

Think about which branches open first, what remains fixed, and what inputs become increasingly expensive.

Two reasons from Chapter 3

Fixed resources become crowded

With limited managers, equipment or space, each additional input may add less output.

Diminishing marginal product

Extra inputs become costlier

The next branch may require overtime, harder-to-fill shifts or more expensive security.

Rising input costs

Higher marginal costs produce an upward-sloping supply curve.

The least intuitive shifter

A clinic can use the same staff for primary-care visits or more highly reimbursed procedures.

What happens to its supply of primary-care visits?

The opportunity cost of primary care rises, so its supply shifts left.

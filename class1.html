The foundation of economics

The measurement challenge

Cost-benefit analysis does not require pretending that everything important has a price. It requires refusing to pretend that things without prices have no value.

A decision people often get wrong · Round 1

A state budget shortfall threatens to close 10 neighborhood health clinics.

Plan A

Four clinics will definitely remain open.

Plan B

40% chance all 10 remain open
60% chance none remain open

Which plan would you recommend? Type A or B—but wait to send.

A new pair of plans · Round 2

The same shortfall still threatens all 10 clinics.

Plan 1

Six clinics will definitely close.

Plan 2

60% chance all 10 close
40% chance none close

Which plan would you recommend? Type 1 or 2—but wait to send.

The reveal

The plans did not change. Only the words changed.

A = 1

Four stay open
Six close

B = 2

40%: all open / none close
60%: none open / all close

Risk preferences may differ. But the same preference should survive a change from “open” to “close.” Put every option into the same language before comparing costs and benefits.

Two separate questions

1

What would actually change?

Additional visits? Who attends? What do they do? What resources are required?

2

How much are those changes worth?

Market prices, behavior, comparisons, surveys—and sometimes honest uncertainty.

Opportunity cost

Every choice contains an “or what?”

If you cannot finish the sentence “We should do X—or do Y instead,” you have not fully described the choice. You have hidden its opportunity cost.

Opportunity cost is the value of the best feasible alternative you give up.

Marginal thinking

“Marginal” is just a slightly forbidding word meaning “the next one.”

The useful question is often not “Is this program good?” but “Should we do a little more—or a little less?”

Zoom poll · One more?

How many additional food-safety inspectors should the county hire?

Each column is one decision. It shows the marginal benefit and marginal cost of hiring that particular inspector—not totals.

Marginal cost ($000s)240250270300340
A · 2B · 3C · 4D · 5

Answer

3

Hire the first three. Stop before the fourth.

Compare each inspector’s marginal benefit with that inspector’s marginal cost. The first three pass; the fourth does not.

Ask whether the next unit adds more benefit than cost—not whether the whole program is “good.”

Nothing happens in isolation

Look in four directions.

  1. 1My other decisionsWhat else will I choose differently?
  2. 2Other actorsWho else will respond or be affected?
  3. 3Other marketsWhat related prices, services or markets adjust?
  4. 4Future possibilitiesWhat becomes easier or harder tomorrow?

Within each direction, keep asking: “And then what?”

Net revenue will fund public transit. Should the city adopt it?

2 · Other actors

I add to congestionAnd then what?Others change routes or departure timesAnd then what?Traffic may move onto residential streets

3 · Other markets

Transit demand risesAnd then what?Crowding and operating costs riseAnd then what?Service, parking prices, rents and locations adjust

4 · Future possibilities

Revenue improves transitAnd then what?Living without a car becomes easierAnd then what?Car ownership, development and future policy choices change

From individual to market demand

At each price, add up everyone’s choice.

0 or 1+0 or 1+0 or 1+ … =market quantity demanded

Market demand is not separate from individual choice. It is the sum of those choices at every possible price.

Read the curve correctly

A demand curve is a set of plans.

It does not say what buyers will purchase at every price. It says what they would purchase at each price, if that were the price they faced.

Only one of those plans becomes today’s observed quantity.

A clean distinction

The hat’s own price changes

Move along the curve

Choose a different plan from the same set.

Something else changes

Shift the curve

The whole set of plans changes.

“Holding other things constant” is not decoration. It is what makes the curve interpretable.

What does the curve’s height mean?

Five buyers’ maximum willingness to pay:

$44$36$29$21$12

At three hats, what does $29 measure?

👍 Total benefit👎 Marginal benefit

The third hat

$44+$36+$29=$109

$29 is the marginal benefit.
$109 is the total willingness to pay.

The height of demand at a quantity is the willingness to pay for the next—or marginal—unit, not the value of all units together.

Useful, but not an all-purpose moral measure

Demand usually describes private marginal willingness to pay.

Ability to pay matters

Low willingness to pay may reflect a tight budget, not a small benefit to well-being.

Other people may benefit

Vaccination, education and clean air can create benefits buyers do not capture.

Choices can mislead

Information, trust, habits and administrative burdens affect what people do.

The unit must be clear

Quality, timing, population and the exact “next unit” all matter.

Demand is evidence about value. It is not the last word on social value.

A practical evidence menu

Look for price variation that teaches you something.

Randomize prices

Pilots, discounts or phased offers. Often the cleanest comparison when feasible.

Use a policy or natural experiment

Find a price change and a credible comparison group.

Study past behavior

Transactions, applications or administrative data—while accounting for other changes.

Compare places or groups

Useful only if their other differences can be handled credibly.

Ask people

Fast and sometimes necessary, but hypothetical answers may differ from real choices.

One last test

Last year, transit fares rose.
Ridership rose too.

Did the law of demand fail?

👍 Yes👎 No

Price and quantity alone are not enough

Demand may have shifted.

Gas prices, service quality, commuting patterns or population could have raised demand while the fare rose.

To estimate a demand curve, we need price variation that was not itself caused by changing demand—and we need a credible comparison.

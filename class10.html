Chapter 21.1–21.2 · The central identity

GDP is one economy viewed three ways

1Total outputThe market value of what is produced
2Total spendingWhat households, firms, government, and foreigners spend on final output
3Total incomeWages and profits earned producing that output

Every dollar spent is income for someone else. Every final product sold is output produced by someone else.

Chapter 21.3 · What GDP captures—and misses

GDP is useful. GDP is not “value.”

CountsMarket productionGovernment servicesPaid care
Misses or mismeasuresUnpaid household workLeisureDistributionEnvironmental damage
Still usefulMore resources for health, education, safety, and choiceStrongly related to life expectancy, schooling, and life satisfaction

GDP measures the resources available to pursue a good life—not the good life itself.

Chapter 22 · Four lenses the chapter uses

What is the chapter’s theory of growth?

MalthusPopulation catches upBefore modern growth, extra output fed more people rather than lifting living standards.
Production functionEquip the workerMore labor, human capital, and physical capital raise output—especially where capital is scarce.
Solow modelDiminishing returnsCapital helps a country catch up, but cannot by itself sustain growth per person forever.
Ideas + institutionsMove the frontierNew recipes and credible rules make sustained growth possible.

These are connected steps in the chapter’s argument: why growth was slow, how countries catch up, and what sustains growth.

Chapter 22.1 · The old growth theory

Why Malthus was a reasonable pessimist

More foodPopulation risesFamilies survive and grow.
→
More peopleFood per person fallsLand and tools are spread more thinly.
→
Living standardsReturn toward subsistenceExtra output does not stay as extra income per person.
The chapter’s revisionMalthus was right about much of history. He was wrong about the future because agricultural and industrial ideas let production outpace population.

Chapter 22.3 · Two different meanings of “more”

Do we add every ingredient—or just one?

Constant returns to scaleDouble L, H, and K → double YReplicate the whole organization: a second identical office, factory, or service team.
Diminishing returnsDouble K alone → less than double YMore machines help, but workers eventually have too many tools to use productively.

Ask: “If a state doubles its budget but not its staff, skills, or management, which story is more plausible?”

Chapter 22.3 · Capital accumulation and the Solow model

Investment is a journey toward a new steady state

1Investment > depreciationCapital per worker rises.
→
2Output risesWorkers have more tools.
→
3Diminishing returnsEach new machine adds less.
→
4Investment = depreciationThe economy reaches a steady state.

A capital push can lift the level of output and create catch-up growth. With labor and skills held fixed, the growth of output per person eventually returns to zero without new ideas.

Chapter 22.3 · Technology is made, not wished into existence

Innovation is production today forgone for output tomorrow

TodayResearchers use labor and capitalThose resources do not produce as many current goods and services.
→
TomorrowNew ideas shift the production function upThe same workers, skills, and machines can produce more valuable output.
→
Over timeGrowth becomes self-reinforcingA richer economy can afford to devote more resources to creating the next idea.

This is the chapter’s opportunity-cost logic: the cost of generating ideas is current output, while the benefit is a larger future frontier.

Chapter 22.3 · The engine of sustained growth

New ideas let the same ingredients produce more

NonexcludableOthers can often copy an idea, so private returns can be smaller than social returns.
Non-depreciatingAn idea does not wear out when we use it.
GenerativeIdeas can create other ideas.
The policy question students put on the tableTechnology can raise productivity. Who receives the gains—and who has the incentive to create the next idea?

Capital accumulation can raise the level of output. Repeated technological progress is what sustains growth in output per person.

Chapter 22.3 · A simple long-run lesson

Investment helps you catch up. Ideas keep moving the frontier.

Step 1Invest in capitalMore machines raise output.
Step 2Diminishing returnsEach additional machine adds less.
Step 3Steady stateInvestment eventually just replaces depreciation.
Step 4Technological progressNew recipes shift the production function up.

This is why “just build more stuff” is not a complete growth strategy.
